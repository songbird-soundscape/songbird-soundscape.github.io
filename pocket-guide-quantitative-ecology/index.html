Before we start: shapes of curves and their families

Almost every figure in this guide is a curve, and most of them belong to a handful of families. A family is one formula with knobs (parameters) you can turn. Turning a knob stretches, shifts or bends the curve, but it stays recognizably the same shape. Learn to recognize the family and you know a lot before reading a single number.

Three questions place almost any curve in its family:

  • Does it keep going, level off, or turn back? Straight lines and exponentials keep going. Saturating and S-shaped curves level off (they have an asymptote, a ceiling they approach but never pass). Bell curves rise and fall again (they are unimodal, with one peak).
  • Does it bend up or down? Bending upward means it speeds up (convex); bending downward means it slows down (concave).
  • What does it look like on log axes? A log scale turns "multiplying" into "adding". An exponential becomes a straight line on a log y axis, and a power law becomes straight when both axes are logged. This is the quickest test there is.

While you drag, the equation fills in with your numbers. Then try the log switches, and try "auto-fit axes" to see how software can hide a change. How the families are related

The families aren't separate species of curve: most are close relatives, linked by a log, a limit or a swap of variables. Each link below has a button that sets up the figure so you can see it.

Exponential and logarithm undo each other

\[y=e^{x}\iff x=\ln y\]

The logarithmic curve is the exponential flipped across the diagonal y = x: one multiplies, the other counts multiplications. That is why a log axis turns an exponential into a straight line.

A power law is an exponential in disguise

\[a\,x^{b}=a\,e^{\,b\ln x}\]

Put ln x where x was and the power law becomes an exponential. Log the x axis and a power law turns into an exponential; log both axes and it becomes a straight line with slope b.

The logarithm is the power law's limit as b → 0

\[\lim_{b\to 0}\frac{x^{b}-1}{b}=\ln x\]

As the exponent shrinks toward zero, a (rescaled) power curve becomes a logarithm. This is the Box–Cox family of transformations: b = 1 leaves data alone, b = 0.5 is a square root, b → 0 is the log. With b = 0.01 and x = 4 the left side is already 1.396, against ln 4 = 1.386.

A straight line is a power law with b = 1, and an exponential up close

\[\begin{gathered}a\,x^{1}=a\,x\\[6pt] e^{bx}\approx 1+bx\ \text{for small } bx\end{gathered}\]

Over a short enough range, almost any smooth curve looks straight. That's why short gradients and short time series so often "show" linear relationships.

The saturating curve is an S-curve on a log x axis

\[\frac{a\,x}{b+x}=\frac{a}{1+e^{-(\ln x-\ln b)}}\]

Michaelis–Menten is the logistic curve with ln x in place of x, steepness 1 and midpoint ln b. Raise x and b to a power n and you get the Hill curve, steeper with larger n; n = 2 is Holling's type III functional response.

An S-curve starts as an exponential

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{a}{1+e^{-b(x-c)}}\approx a\,e^{\,b(x-c)}\\\text{when } x\ll c\end{gathered}\]

Well before the midpoint, the logistic is indistinguishable from exponential growth, as the yeast case shows. On a log y axis its early part is a straight line.

The bell is e raised to a parabola, and the S-curve's slope is a bell

\[\begin{gathered}y=a\,e^{-\frac{(x-c)^2}{2b^2}}\\[6pt] \frac{d}{dx}\frac{a}{1+e^{-b(x-c)}}=b\,y\Bigl(1-\frac{y}{a}\Bigr)\end{gathered}\]

Put a downward parabola in the exponent and you get the Gaussian; on a log y axis the bell becomes that parabola again. Going the other way, the rate of change of a logistic is a hump that peaks at the midpoint, and the running total (integral) of a Gaussian is an S-curve (the probit).

The skewed bell contains the Gaussian

\[s=0,\ k=2\ \Rightarrow\ y=3\,e^{-\frac{(x-c)^2}{b^2}}\]

With no skew and peakedness 2, the two-piece bell is a Gaussian (with width b/√2 in the Gaussian's units). k = 1 gives the Laplace (double-exponential) shape, the simplest fat-tailed kernel.

The community table

Everything starts as a table: one row per sample (a plot, a trap, a quadrat), one column per taxon, and a count in each cell. Sometimes a row is counted to a fixed total (say the first 300 individuals identified); sometimes it is everything found in a plot or trap. Most cells are zero, and the zeros carry information too.

Before anything is compared, each row is usually turned into proportions, so a sample counted to 300 individuals and one counted to 150 can be set side by side. A proportion is the count divided by the row total:

\[p_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}}{\sum_{j} x_{ij}}\]

Read it aloud. This taxon's count, divided by everything counted in that sample.

Familiar relative. A percentage divided by 100, or the bar heights of a histogram rescaled to add up to 1. Once a row sums to 1 it is a probability distribution: the chance that a randomly chosen individual from that sample belongs to each taxon.

With numbers. Sample A has 40 individuals of one species out of 100, so p = 40/100 = 0.40, meaning 40% of the individuals or a 0.4 chance per individual.

Diversity in one sample: Hill numbers

"How diverse is this sample?" has no single answer, because it depends on how much weight rare taxa get. Hill numbers make that weight a dial, the order q:

\[{}^{q}\!D=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{S} p_j^{\,q}\Bigr)^{1/(1-q)}\]
Dissect this equation
\[{}^{q}\!D=\Bigl(\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_{j}}\color{#3d7bd9}{p_j^{\,q}}\Bigr)^{\color{#d0583c}{1/(1-q)}}\]
pjq
each taxon's share raised to the power q. If q is below 1, small shares are boosted relative to big ones; above 1, they shrink toward nothing
Σj
add those powered shares over all taxa
1/(1−q)
an exponent that undoes the powering, so the answer comes out in units of "number of taxa"

Read it aloud. Raise every share to the power q, add them up, then take the (1 − q)th root.

Familiar relative. A Hill number is one over an average share, and q picks which kind of average: harmonic (q = 0), geometric (q = 1) or arithmetic (q = 2). These are the power means from school. Diversity is "one over how common the typical individual's taxon is".

\[\begin{gathered}{}^{q}\!D=\frac{1}{\Bigl(\sum_j p_j\,p_j^{\,q-1}\Bigr)^{1/(q-1)}}\\[6pt]=\frac{1}{\text{mean share, weighted by }p}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. Two taxa at 0.75 and 0.25. At q = 0: 2 taxa. At q = 2: Σp² = 0.5625 + 0.0625 = 0.625, so ²D = 1/0.625 = 1.6. At q = 1: e0.562 = 1.75. The more weight on the dominant taxon, the fewer "effective" taxa.

At q = 0 every taxon counts as 1, so 0D is plain richness S. At q = 1 the formula has a limit, the exponential of Shannon entropy H. At q = 2 it is the inverse of Simpson's concentration.

\[\begin{gathered}{}^{1}\!D=e^{H},\;H=-\sum_j p_j\ln p_j\\[6pt]{}^{2}\!D=\frac{1}{\sum_j p_j^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#d0583c}{H}=-\sum_j \color{#3d7bd9}{p_j}\color{#c08f00}{\ln p_j}\qquad {}^{1}\!D=\color{#2f9a55}{e^{H}}\\[6pt]\color{#8a5cc4}{\sum_j p_j^2}\qquad {}^{2}\!D=\color{#2f9a55}{\frac{1}{\sum_j p_j^2}}\\[6pt]\text{Gini–Simpson}=1-\color{#8a5cc4}{\sum_j p_j^2}\qquad J=\frac{\color{#d0583c}{H}}{\ln S}\end{gathered}\]
pj
the chance a random individual is taxon j
−ln pj
the "surprise" of seeing taxon j: 0 for a taxon that is everything, large for a rare one (−ln 0.01 = 4.6)

the average surprise, each surprise weighted by how often it happens. Units are "nats" with ln (bits if you use log₂)

Read it aloud. Shannon averages the surprise of each individual and exponentiates it to give a number of taxa; Simpson asks how often two random individuals match and inverts it.

Familiar relative. H is an expected value, like the mean of a die roll Σp·x, where each "x" is a surprise. Simpson's Σp² is the probability of a match, like the birthday problem. Gini–Simpson is also a sum of variances: each taxon is a yes/no variable with variance p(1 − p), and those add up to 1 − Σp². Turning H and Σp² into ¹D and ²D is what puts them on the same "number of taxa" scale as richness.

\[\begin{gathered}\text{mean}=\sum_x p(x)\,x\\[6pt]\sum_j p_j(1-p_j)=1-\sum_j p_j^2\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. Shares 0.75 and 0.25. Surprises: −ln 0.75 = 0.29 and −ln 0.25 = 1.39, so H = 0.75 × 0.29 + 0.25 × 1.39 = 0.56 and ¹D = e0.56 = 1.75. Match chance Σp² = 0.5625 + 0.0625 = 0.625, so ²D = 1/0.625 = 1.6 and Gini–Simpson = 0.375. Pielou J = 0.56/ln 2 = 0.81.

Every Hill number is in units of effective number of equally common taxa. A sample with 1D = 6 is as diverse as one with six equally abundant taxa, whatever its real richness.

Sampling design: plots, transects and what they do to the analysis

Every number in this guide comes from samples, and the way they were taken is built into every result. Two field crews in the same forest, one using small random plots and one using a transect across the slope, will produce different α, β and γ, different ordinations and different p-values. Neither is wrong; they are answering different questions.

Plot size (grain)

Bigger plots catch more species, so α rises with plot area, roughly as the power law S = cAz from the curves chapter. Because γ for the whole study changes much less, β = γ/α falls as plots grow. Beta diversity is partly a property of your quadrat size, not just of the landscape. Compare studies only at matching grain, or rarefy to a common area.

Plot shape

A long thin plot of the same area crosses more ground, and therefore more patches and more of the gradient, than a square one. It usually records more species, but mixes communities inside one sample. Squares and circles give the most "pure" sample of a patch.

How many plots, and where

  • Simple random placement is unbiased, but can leave rare habitats unsampled by chance.
  • Systematic grids cover space evenly and are easy to relocate. If the landscape has a regular pattern (ridges, furrows), a grid can fall in step with it.
  • Stratified random placement puts a set number of plots in each habitat or band of the gradient, which guarantees coverage. This is the usual choice when groups will be compared.
  • Belt transects are a line of contiguous plots. Run across a gradient, a transect captures it efficiently: the ordination lines the plots up in gradient order and the Mantel r (below) is strong. Run along a contour, the same effort sees no gradient at all and finds fewer species.

The species accumulation curve (a saturating curve) tells you whether you have enough plots: when it flattens, extra plots add few new species.

What you measure in each plot

Counts of stems (density), percent cover, basal area, frequency (the share of subplots where a species occurs) or presence only. Each gives a differently shaped table. Cover and basal area weight big plants; counts weight small, numerous ones; presence weights everything equally. Pick the distance to match: Jaccard for presence, Bray–Curtis or Hellinger for abundance.

Neighbours aren't independent

Plots close together tend to be similar simply because they are close (spatial autocorrelation). The Mantel test measures this. It correlates the matrix of geographic distances with the matrix of community distances:

\[r_M=\operatorname{corr}\left(d_{\text{space}},\;d_{\text{community}}\right)\]

\[\color{#d0583c}{r_M}=\operatorname{corr}\left(\color{#3d7bd9}{d_{\text{space}}},\;\color{#c08f00}{d_{\text{community}}}\right)\]

Familiar relative. This is Pearson's correlation r, applied to the pairs instead of the plots. Because pairs share plots they aren't independent, so its significance is tested by shuffling plot labels (a permutation test), not with the usual t-table. A positive rM is distance decay: nearby plots are more alike.

With numbers. 12 plots give 66 pairs. If pairs far apart on the ground tend to be the most different, rM might be 0.5. Along a contour, where distance on the ground doesn't change the habitat, it falls near 0.

A strong rM in a transect is expected: that is the gradient. But if plots are treated as independent replicates when they are really clustered neighbours, PERMANOVA and ANOSIM p-values come out too small. This is the pseudoreplication problem (Hurlbert 1984). Spread replicates out, or test with restricted permutations.

Nested plots

Whittaker-style nested designs put small subplots inside larger ones (for example 1, 10 and 100 m²). That gives a species–area curve from every site and lets you compare richness at any grain.

When the sampler sets its own footprint

Many samplers have a footprint you didn't draw. A light trap pulls moths from a distance that depends on the lamp and the terrain; an acoustic recorder hears birds out to a radius that depends on the species' songs and the vegetation; eDNA in a stream carries traces from upstream. Each species can have its own effective area, so the "plot" differs by taxon. Palynologists have worked this out for pollen, where a basin's "relevant source area" depends on its size and on how far each taxon's pollen travels (Sugita 1994). Whatever the sampler, comparing it with plot data is a question of grain, and every lesson in this chapter applies.

PCA: rotating the axes to catch the most variance

NMDS, one step at a time

Non-metric multidimensional scaling doesn't solve an equation; it searches. It starts from some configuration of points, then repeats three moves:

  1. Measure the distance z between every pair on the plot.
  2. Fit the best monotone step line from the original distances to the plot distances. This is isotonic regression, here by the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm. The fitted values ẑ respect only rank order: if pair 1 is more different than pair 2 in the data, ẑ1 ≥ ẑ2.
  3. Move every point to shrink the gap between z and ẑ (here by the SMACOF update).

Stress, random starts and noise

Stress measures one thing: how faithfully the picture keeps the rank order of the original distances. Clarke's (1993) rough guide is below 0.05 excellent, below 0.1 good, below 0.2 usable with care, and above 0.3 close to arbitrary.

Three cautions follow from the mechanics:

  • Local minima. Different starts can end at different stresses. The histogram shows the final stress from 30 random starts. Always keep the best of many, and check that the best repeats.
  • Noise and sample size. More noise, or more samples, usually means more stress, so a fixed cut-off penalizes big datasets (Dexter et al. 2018).
  • Low stress is not separation. A plot can map its distances perfectly and still show no structure; "Do the groups differ?" below shows how to check.

Procrustes: comparing two plots

Two ordinations of the same samples, say from a direct census and from an indirect sample such as trap catches or eDNA, can't simply be laid on top of each other, because NMDS axes have no fixed direction or scale. (In the simulation, the indirect sample has the usual faults of such methods: some species are caught far more readily than others, some can only be named to genus or family, and strays arrive from the wider region.) Procrustes analysis removes everything that doesn't matter, then measures what's left:

  1. Centre both configurations on the origin.
  2. Scale each to a total sum of squares of 1.
  3. Rotate, and reflect if that helps, the second onto the first to minimize the squared distances between matching points.
\[\begin{gathered}m^2=1-\Bigl(\max_{\mathbf Q}\operatorname{tr}\,\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf Y\mathbf Q\Bigr)^2\\[6pt]r=\sqrt{1-m^2}\end{gathered}\]

Q

Familiar relative. This is Pearson's correlation generalized to points in a plane. Pearson's r is the average product of standardized x and y. Procrustes r is the summed products of standardized coordinates of matching points, after the best rotation. m² plays the role of 1 − r².

Modelling taxa and community types: binomial and multinomial regression

So far every question has been about distances. Regression asks a different kind of question: given where a sample sits, how likely is something? For example, how likely is a taxon to be present, or the sample to belong to a given community? The answers are probabilities between 0 and 1, so ordinary straight-line regression won't do. It would happily predict a probability of 1.3 or −0.2. These models fit a straight line on a different scale, the log-odds, and bend it back into the 0–1 range with the S-curve from the first chapter.

1. Binomial (logistic) regression: is the taxon here?

Each sample along a gradient records a taxon as present (1) or absent (0). Logistic regression models the probability of presence, p, by fitting a straight line to its log-odds:

\[\begin{gathered}\ln\frac{p}{1-p}=\beta_0+\beta_1x\\[6pt]p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\beta_0+\beta_1x)}}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#d0583c}{\ln\frac{p}{1-p}}=\color{#3d7bd9}{\beta_0}+\color{#2f9a55}{\beta_1x}\qquad p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(\color{#3d7bd9}{\beta_0}+\color{#2f9a55}{\beta_1x})}}\]

Read it aloud. The log of the odds of presence is a straight line in x; undo the log and you get an S-shaped probability.

Familiar relative. This is ordinary linear regression, done on the log-odds scale instead of the raw scale. Odds are the betting kind ("4 to 1"), and eβ₁ is an odds ratio, the same quantity reported in medical studies ("smokers have 3 times the odds"). The link between the straight line and the probability is called the link function.

\[\text{odds}=\frac{p}{1-p}\qquad \text{odds ratio per unit } x=e^{\beta_1}\]

With numbers. p = 0.8 gives odds 0.8/0.2 = 4 and log-odds ln 4 = 1.39. If β₁ = 2, moving one unit along x multiplies the odds by e² ≈ 7.4, so odds of 4 become 29.6, or p = 0.97.

On the probability scale that line becomes the S-curve: the taxon gets steadily more likely along the gradient. Real taxa usually have an optimum, though, and are rare at both ends. Adding a squared term makes the log-odds a downward parabola, which turns the probability into a bell. This is the Gaussian-logit model (ter Braak & Looman 1986):

\[\begin{gathered}\ln\frac{p}{1-p}=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2\\[6pt]u=-\frac{\beta_1}{2\beta_2},\ \ t=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2\beta_2}}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\ln\frac{p}{1-p}=\color{#3d7bd9}{\beta_0}+\color{#2f9a55}{\beta_1x}+\color{#d0583c}{\beta_2x^2}\\[6pt]\color{#c08f00}{u}=-\frac{\color{#2f9a55}{\beta_1}}{2\color{#d0583c}{\beta_2}},\qquad \color{#8a5cc4}{t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-2\color{#d0583c}{\beta_2}}}\end{gathered}\]

Familiar relative. Completing the square from school algebra turns β0 + β1x + β2x² into a peak height minus (x − u)²/(2t²). That is exactly the exponent of a normal (Gaussian) curve, so u plays the mean and t the standard deviation.

\[\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2=\text{peak}-\frac{(x-u)^2}{2t^2}\]

With numbers. With β1 = 26.6 and β2 = −26.3: u = 26.6/(2 × 26.3) = 0.51 and t = 1/√(2 × 26.3) = 0.14, close to the true 0.50 and 0.15.

Two ecologically meaningful numbers fall straight out of the coefficients: the optimum u, where the taxon is most likely, and the tolerance t, its niche width. The fit works by maximum likelihood (here by iteratively reweighted least squares): it finds the coefficients that make the observed 0s and 1s most probable. The deviance (−2 × log-likelihood) measures what's left unexplained. AIC adds 2 per coefficient as a penalty, and the lower AIC wins.

2. The counting model: every share is a binomial draw

Counting a sample to a fixed total is itself a binomial (and, across all taxa, multinomial) experiment. Each of the n individuals belongs to taxon j with probability p. The count you get therefore scatters around np, and the observed share scatters around p with a standard error:

\[\begin{gathered}X\sim\text{Binomial}(n,p)\\[6pt]\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\end{gathered}\]

3. Multinomial regression: which community is this?

With more than two outcomes (here four community types, A to D) the binomial model generalizes to the multinomial, or softmax, model. Each community gets its own straight-line score η, built from the sample's position (here its first two PCoA axes on Hellinger distance). The scores are turned into probabilities that add to 1:

\[\begin{gathered}\eta_k=\beta_{0k}+\beta_{1k}z_1+\beta_{2k}z_2\\[6pt]P(\text{community }k)=\frac{e^{\eta_k}}{\sum_{j}e^{\eta_j}}\end{gathered}\]

When a simple rule misbehaves: the logistic map and chaos

Everything so far has described samples as snapshots. Samples repeated through time, from a monitoring programme or down a sediment core, are also a sequence: each sample comes after the one before it. This chapter is about the simplest model of change through time, and about the surprise hiding in it. A one-line rule with no randomness at all can produce a sequence that never repeats and can't be forecast far ahead. That is what chaos means.

1. The map

Think of x as the share of the landscape held by one community (0 = gone, 1 = everywhere), counted once per generation. Each generation it grows by a factor r, but only into the room that's left:

\[x_{t+1}=r\,x_t(1-x_t)\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#2f9a55}{x_{t+1}}=\color{#d0583c}{r}\,\color{#3d7bd9}{x_t}\,\color{#c08f00}{(1-x_t)}\]

Familiar relative. Two relatives. The product x(1 − x) is the same hump as the binomial variance p(1 − p) from the regression chapter: zero at both ends, largest at one half. And the map is the step-by-step version of the smooth logistic growth curve, the S-curve of the first chapter. The smooth curve can never overshoot its ceiling; the stepped version can, because it only "looks" once per generation, and overshooting is where all the strange behaviour comes from.

\[\begin{gathered}\frac{dN}{dt}=rN\Bigl(1-\frac{N}{K}\Bigr)\\\text{smooth: always an S-curve}\\[8pt]x_{t+1}=r\,x_t(1-x_t)\\\text{stepped: can oscillate}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. r = 2.8, starting at x = 0.2: next is 2.8 × 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.448, then 2.8 × 0.448 × 0.552 = 0.692, then 0.596, 0.674, 0.615, 0.663. It overshoots and undershoots, closing in on 0.643.

That is the whole model: one equation, one knob. Run it by feeding each answer back in. The cobweb plot does this with a ruler. Go up from xt to the hump (that's xt+1), across to the diagonal (which turns the answer into the next starting point), up or down to the hump again, and so on.

\[x^*=1-\frac1r\qquad f'(x^*)=2-r\]

Familiar relative. This is the same idea as a ball in a bowl. If each step shrinks the nudge (the slope is between −1 and 1), the ball settles. If the slope is beyond −1, each step flips the nudge to the other side and enlarges it, so the ball rocks ever wider: that happens once r passes 3, where 2 − r passes −1, and it is exactly where the 2-cycle is born. A negative slope means overshooting; a slope beyond −1 means the overshoots grow.

3. Sensitive dependence: why chaos can't be forecast far

Start two runs with a tiny difference, say a measurement error in the fourth decimal place, and watch how far apart they drift. One number summarises it, the Lyapunov exponent:

\[\lambda=\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^{n}\ln\bigl|r\,(1-2x_t)\bigr|\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#d0583c}{\lambda}=\color{#c08f00}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}}\color{#3d7bd9}{\ln\bigl\lvert r\,(1-2x_t)\bigr\rvert}\]
ln|r(1 − 2xt)|
the slope of the map where the trajectory is right now, on a log scale. Above 0 means a small error grows at this step; below 0 means it shrinks

Read it aloud. Average, over many steps, the log of how steep the map is where you are standing.

Familiar relative. Taking the log of a product of multipliers turns it into a sum, then dividing by n gives a mean: λ is the log of a geometric mean, like the geometric mean growth rate of a population. Negative λ: errors die away and the future is predictable (steady states and cycles). Positive λ: errors grow exponentially, the signature of chaos. It is also why forecasting fails so quickly: doubling your measurement precision only buys about ln 2 / λ extra steps.

\[\begin{gathered}\varepsilon_t\approx\varepsilon_0\,e^{\lambda t}\\[6pt]t_{\text{forecast}}\approx\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{\text{tolerance}}{\varepsilon_0}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. At r = 4, λ = ln 2 = 0.693: errors double every step. At r = 3.9, λ ≈ 0.50. Two runs starting 0.0001 apart differ by more than 0.1 after 16 steps; starting 0.000001 apart (100 times more precise) only buys 22. At r = 2.8, λ = ln 0.8 = −0.22: errors shrink by a fifth each step.

When λ is negative (steady states and cycles), the two runs converge: the error is forgotten. When it's positive, the error grows roughly exponentially until the runs are unrelated. Near-term forecasts still work, but each extra step ahead costs a fixed amount of precision. This decay of forecasting skill with horizon is the practical test for chaos in real data (Sugihara & May 1990): noisy data forecast badly at every horizon, while chaotic data forecast well one step ahead and steadily worse after that.

4. How finely would a layered record need to be sampled?

Suppose you slice a core of lake mud or peat layer by layer, top to bottom, with no gaps. Each slice is a slice of time, and the remains in it are a blend of everything that lived while that layer was laid down. Whether the dynamics survive in the slices depends on one ratio: how many community "generations" each slice blends together.

The years in a slice follow from three numbers: slice thickness × compaction (original thickness ÷ present thickness) ÷ accumulation rate. A "generation" here is the turnover time of the community, how long one round of the rule takes, which is rarely known for a past community. So it's a knob. Published compaction ratios and accumulation rates vary widely, so treat the defaults as placeholders and set your own.

The widget runs the logistic map one step per generation for as long as the seam represents. It then averages the community's share within each slice and counts a fixed number of individuals from it (the binomial noise from the regression chapter). Finally it asks how well each sample predicts the next ones. The test is simplex projection (Sugihara & May 1990). It finds the past moments that looked most like now and sees what came next. It uses the first half of the column to forecast the second, and the skill is the correlation between forecast and observed (1 = perfect, 0 = no better than guessing).

Try this
  • Start with r = 3.9 in section 1. With the defaults (a 20-year generation, 10 mm slices), each slice blends 5 generations. Forecast skill is near zero: the chaos is there in the living community but invisible in the core.
  • Thin the slices until each holds about one generation (2 mm here). Skill jumps to near 1 one step ahead and falls with horizon, the signature of chaos.
  • Lengthen the generation to 100 years. Now 10 mm slices are fine. The required slice thickness scales with how slowly the community turns over.
  • Set r = 3.2 with 2 mm slices. Skill stays high at every horizon: a cycle is perfectly predictable.

This is a toy: one community, constant accumulation, perfect layering. In a real core, accumulation rate and compaction vary down the column, burrowing animals and roots mix the layers, and dates are sparse. Each of these blurs or warps the time axis further, so the real requirement is stricter than the widget shows.

5. From one population to a sequence of samples

The logistic map follows one population against its own ceiling. Which community holds a landscape is a question about several communities competing, so real tests use models with more than one variable. The lessons carry over, though, and they matter for reading a sequence of samples:

  • Irregular does not mean random. A fully deterministic system can give a sequence that looks like noise.
  • Irregular does not mean chaotic, either. Outside forcing (climate, sea level), directional succession and random disturbance also make irregular sequences. So do systems with two alternative stable states that get knocked between them. A system near the tipping point between two states often flickers, making brief visits to the other state before it switches for good (Scheffer et al. 2009). Flickering, cycles and chaos leave different fingerprints.
  • Tests need long, even series. Detecting chaos usually takes on the order of a hundred or more evenly spaced steps. A core gives fewer samples, unevenly spaced in time, each blending years to centuries, and many indirect samplers mix local and regional sources. A monitoring programme samples evenly but rarely runs long enough. All of that limits what the record can show, as section 4 shows.

How the formulas are related

Nearly every index in this guide is a familiar statistic in a new setting. The table pairs each with its relative from an introductory statistics course. The dissections under each formula go into more detail.

One identity carries most of the weight, and it's worth seeing work with your own eyes: the spread of a set of numbers can be measured from their pairwise differences alone, without ever computing the mean.

\[\begin{gathered}\underbrace{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}_{\text{sum of squares}}=\frac1n\underbrace{\sum_{a\lt b}(x_a-x_b)^2}_{\text{all pairwise gaps}^2}\\[6pt]s^2=\frac{SS}{n-1}\\[6pt]s=\sqrt{s^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#3d7bd9}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\color{#c08f00}{\frac1n}\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_{a\lt b}(x_a-x_b)^2}\\[6pt]\color{#d0583c}{s^2}=\frac{\color{#3d7bd9}{SS}}{n-1},\qquad s=\sqrt{s^2}\end{gathered}\]
Σ(xi − x̄)²
the sum of squares: each value's gap from the mean, squared and added (the blue lines in the figure)
Σa<b(xa − xb)²
every pair's gap, squared and added (the gold arcs); with n values there are n(n − 1)/2 pairs
1/n
dividing by n makes the two totals equal, because each value takes part in n − 1 pairs
s²
the variance: the sum of squares averaged over n − 1 degrees of freedom; s, its square root, is the standard deviation

Read it aloud. The total squared spread around the mean equals the total squared gaps between all pairs, divided by the number of values.

Familiar relative. This links the textbook variance to distance matrices. Replace the plain gaps (xa − xb) with any distance dab and you have PERMANOVA's total sum of squares, and the trace of PCoA's B matrix. That is why both can work with Bray–Curtis or any other distance.

\[SS_T=\frac1n\sum_{a\lt b}d_{ab}^2\quad(\text{PERMANOVA})\]

With numbers. Values 1, 2, 3 and 6 (mean 3): squared gaps from the mean 4 + 1 + 0 + 9 = 14. Squared pairwise gaps 1 + 4 + 25 + 1 + 16 + 9 = 56, and 56/4 = 14. Variance s² = 14/3 = 4.67, so s = 2.16.

Because of it, variance, standard deviation, ANOVA and regression can all be rebuilt on a distance matrix. Swap the plain differences for Bray–Curtis distances and you get PERMANOVA (splitting the sum of squares) and PCoA (the same sum of squares, shared out among axes by eigenvalue).

  • Add a point far out (raise "outlier"). Both sums jump together, because squaring makes far points count heavily. That is why stress and PERMANOVA are sensitive to outliers while rank-based ANOSIM is not.
