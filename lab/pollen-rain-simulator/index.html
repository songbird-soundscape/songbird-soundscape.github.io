Scale 1 · micrometres

The grain: how fast it falls

Fall speed is the single grain property that drives every dispersal model further down this page. In still air a small grain falls at the speed where drag balances weight, which is Stokes' law. Size counts twice (weight grows with d³, drag with d), so a maize grain falls about ten times faster than a birch grain and seldom travels far.

Scale 2 · millimetres

Capture at the ovule: pine and Ginkgo

Gymnosperm ovules catch pollen in a pollination drop, a bead of fluid secreted from the micropyle and later withdrawn. In Pinus the ovule faces down and the drop hangs below it; buoyant saccate grains float up through the drop into the ovule. Ginkgo grains have no sacci and do not float, so capture depends on the drop being drawn back in. Run the season, then change the ovule orientation or the pollen mix to see which combinations work.

The animation is a qualitative model: grains move by buoyancy relative to the drop fluid, and a grain left on the surface of a fast-shrinking drop can stick and be stranded. It reproduces the pattern Leslie (2010) found experimentally, where flotation in downward-facing ovules preferentially captures saccate pollen. The drop itself (sugars, proteins, its role in pollen germination) is not modelled.

The core: eleven thousand years

The same landscape model, run on a Holocene vegetation history: open steppe giving way to birch and pine, then hazel and mixed oak forest, alder on wet ground, beech late, and clearance for farming. Each level is counted under the site, wind and sediment settings above, and every level is as old as its depth. Solid silhouettes are the pollen count; the dashed line is the true land cover on the map.

Hollow outlines are ×10 exaggerations for minor taxa. Things to look for: Tilia forms far more of the forest than its pollen shows; Alnus and Pinus overshoot; cereal pollen hardly registers until fields reach the basin edge; and with the dry phase switched on, robust Lactuceae and fern spores spike with the indeterminable grains.

What the model does

Production

Each taxon releases pollen in proportion to its cover times a pollen productivity estimate (PPE) relative to Poaceae = 1. Tree values follow the European syntheses (Broström et al. 2008; Mazier et al. 2012). Values marked ° are illustrative guesses, mostly for spores, aquatics and Populus.

Dispersal

Sutton's plume as used by Prentice (1985) and Sugita (1993): the share still airborne at distance z is g(z) = exp(−b·zn/2) with b = 4vg / (n·u·√π·Cz). Deposition per m² is −g′(z)/2πz. Heavy grains with a high fall speed vg (Fagus, cereals) drop close to the source.

Wind and canopy

A steady prevailing wind weights upwind cells by 1 + a·cos(θ). Under a canopy, understorey plants release into slow trunk-space air, and a site inside closed forest receives less pollen from beyond ~150 m (Tauber 1965's trunk-space, canopy and rain-out components).

The basin

A lake collects over its whole surface, so no source is counted closer than the basin radius. Small basins are dominated by the vegetation at their edge; large lakes average a wide region (Jacobson & Bradshaw 1981). Cells inside the basin become open water.

Preservation

Each taxon has a corrosion susceptibility based on Havinga's oxidation series. The surviving share is exp(−3s⁶)·exp(−6E·s²·√t), where E is the environment's oxidation rate and t the age in thousands of years. Thin-walled grains (Populus, Ceratophyllum) are lost even in good conditions; robust ones (Lactuceae, fern spores) become over-represented as conditions worsen.

Grain physics

Fall speed v = m·g / (3π·μ·d·χ), with mass from a body of density ρ plus two sacci at 0.1 g/cm³, d the diameter of a sphere of equal volume, μ the viscosity of air and a Schiller–Naumann correction when the Reynolds number nears 1. Densities and shape factors for the presets are set so the model matches measured fall speeds.

Inference

The REVEALS-style column divides each count by PPE × the deposition a uniform landscape would deliver to this basin (Sugita 2007). It assumes even vegetation, no directional wind and perfect preservation, so its error shows what the core cannot tell you on its own.
