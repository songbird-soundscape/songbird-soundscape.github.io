Six Slices of Earth

Six complete circles of latitude, each chosen to cross one continent's most revealing geology, flattened into strips and coloured with the International Chronostratigraphic Chart. Switch on a layer in the ideal column and watch where it survives, where it breaks off, and where it never was.

What you see is the surface. A layer missing from a strip may have been eroded away, never deposited, or simply buried beneath younger rock.

Underground, data is uneven. Dated buried layers come from compiled borehole and outcrop columns, which exist mainly in the Americas. Elsewhere only undated sediment thickness is known.

The ocean floor is young. Ocean crust is recycled at subduction zones, so most of it is younger than the Jurassic. The oldest on these circles, under the Ionian Sea, is Triassic.

How to read a slice

Strip: the flattened map, coloured by the age of the surface rock or ocean floor. A unit dated to several periods is drawn as thin bands, youngest on top.

Certainty: dark = dated to one period, mid = two or three, pale = four or more; blue ticks = a borehole/outcrop column exists here.

Relief: elevation and ocean depth, exaggerated vertically.

Below the surface: dated layers from stratigraphic columns where available, stacked youngest to oldest to 6 km (thicknesses approximate: the thickest unit of each period); elsewhere undated sediment (tan) above crystalline basement (grey), from CRUST1.0.

Why some layers vanish

Rocks only form where something accumulates, and survive only where they escape erosion and subduction. Unconformities, the gaps between layers, are the norm. The Grand Canyon's walls skip over a billion years at the Great Unconformity.

Shields like the Kaapvaal and Yilgarn expose Archean rock because younger cover was stripped away; basins like Sichuan or the Paraná preserve thick young layers; mountain belts tilt everything up to the light.
