Before we start: shapes of curves and their families

Almost every figure in this course is a curve, and most of them belong to a handful of families. A family is one formula with knobs (parameters) you can turn. Turning a knob stretches, shifts or bends the curve, but it stays recognizably the same shape. Learn to recognize the family and you know a lot before reading a single number.

Three questions place almost any curve in its family:

  • Does it keep going, level off, or turn back? Straight lines and exponentials keep going. Saturating and S-shaped curves level off (they have an asymptote, a ceiling they approach but never pass). Bell curves rise and fall again (they are unimodal, with one peak).
  • Does it bend up or down? Bending upward means it speeds up (convex); bending downward means it slows down (concave).
  • What does it look like on log axes? A log scale turns "multiplying" into "adding". An exponential becomes a straight line on a log y axis, and a power law becomes straight when both axes are logged. This is the quickest test there is.

Pick a family below, turn its knobs, and try the log switches. Each card says where that shape turns up later in the course.

The community table

Everything starts as a table: one row per sample (a plot, a peel, a slide), one column per taxon, and a count in each cell. In palynology a row is a slide counted to a fixed sum; in vegetation it is a quadrat or plot. Most cells are zero, and the zeros carry information too.

Before anything is compared, each row is usually turned into proportions, so a sample counted to 300 grains and one counted to 150 can be set side by side. A proportion is the count divided by the row total:

\[p_{ij}=\frac{x_{ij}}{\sum_{j} x_{ij}}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#3d7bd9}{p_{ij}}=\frac{\color{#c08f00}{x_{ij}}}{\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_{j}x_{ij}}}\]
pij
the share of sample i that belongs to taxon j, between 0 and 1
xij
the raw count of taxon j in sample i
Σj xij
add across the row: the total grains counted in sample i

Read it aloud. This taxon's count, divided by everything counted in that sample.

Familiar relative. A percentage divided by 100, or the bar heights of a histogram rescaled to add up to 1. Once a row sums to 1 it is a probability distribution: the chance that a randomly chosen grain from that slide belongs to each taxon.

With numbers. Sample A has 40 Lycospora out of 100 grains, so p = 40/100 = 0.40, meaning 40% of the grains or a 0.4 chance per grain.

Here i is the sample and j the taxon. That choice already shapes everything after it: proportions forget the count size, and presence–absence forgets the abundances.

Diversity in one sample: Hill numbers

"How diverse is this sample?" has no single answer, because it depends on how much weight rare taxa get. Hill numbers make that weight a dial, the order q:

\[{}^{q}\!D=\Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{S} p_j^{\,q}\Bigr)^{1/(1-q)}\]
Dissect this equation
\[{}^{q}\!D=\Bigl(\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_{j}}\color{#3d7bd9}{p_j^{\,q}}\Bigr)^{\color{#d0583c}{1/(1-q)}}\]
pjq
each taxon's share raised to the power q. If q is below 1, small shares are boosted relative to big ones; above 1, they shrink toward nothing
Σj
add those powered shares over all taxa
1/(1−q)
an exponent that undoes the powering, so the answer comes out in units of "number of taxa"

Read it aloud. Raise every share to the power q, add them up, then take the (1 − q)th root.

Familiar relative. A Hill number is one over an average share, and q picks which kind of average: harmonic (q = 0), geometric (q = 1) or arithmetic (q = 2). These are the power means from school. Diversity is "one over how common the typical grain's taxon is".

\[\begin{gathered}{}^{q}\!D=\frac{1}{\Bigl(\sum_j p_j\,p_j^{\,q-1}\Bigr)^{1/(q-1)}}\\[6pt]=\frac{1}{\text{mean share, weighted by }p}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. Two taxa at 0.75 and 0.25. At q = 0: 2 taxa. At q = 2: Σp² = 0.5625 + 0.0625 = 0.625, so ²D = 1/0.625 = 1.6. At q = 1: e0.562 = 1.75. The more weight on the dominant taxon, the fewer "effective" taxa.

At q = 0 every taxon counts as 1, so 0D is plain richness S. At q = 1 the formula has a limit, the exponential of Shannon entropy H. At q = 2 it is the inverse of Simpson's concentration.

\[\begin{gathered}{}^{1}\!D=e^{H},\;H=-\sum_j p_j\ln p_j\\[6pt]{}^{2}\!D=\frac{1}{\sum_j p_j^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#d0583c}{H}=-\sum_j \color{#3d7bd9}{p_j}\color{#c08f00}{\ln p_j}\\[6pt]\color{#8a5cc4}{\sum_j p_j^2}\end{gathered}\]
pj
the chance a random grain is taxon j
−ln pj
the "surprise" of seeing taxon j: large for rare taxa, near zero for the dominant
H
the average surprise, each surprise weighted by how often it happens
Σpj²
the chance that two grains drawn at random (with replacement) are the same taxon

Read it aloud. Shannon: the average, over grains, of how surprising each grain's taxon is. Simpson: the chance two random grains match.

Familiar relative. Shannon's H is an expected value, the mean of −ln p weighted by p, exactly like the mean of a die roll Σ p·x. Gini–Simpson 1 − Σp² is a sum of variances: each taxon is a yes/no (Bernoulli) variable with variance p(1 − p), and those add up to 1 − Σp².

\[\begin{gathered}\text{mean}=\sum_x p(x)\,x\\[6pt]\sum_j p_j(1-p_j)=1-\sum_j p_j^2\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. With shares 0.75 and 0.25, the surprises are −ln 0.75 = 0.29 and −ln 0.25 = 1.39. Weighted: 0.75 × 0.29 + 0.25 × 1.39 = 0.56 = H. The match probability is 0.625, so two random grains are the same taxon 62.5% of the time.

Every Hill number is in units of effective number of equally common taxa. A sample with 1D = 6 is as diverse as one with six equally abundant taxa, whatever its real richness.

The bar chart shows what q does: the grey bars are each taxon's proportion; the coloured bars are its share of the sum Σpq. At q = 0 all shares are equal. As q rises, the dominants take over and the rare taxa fade from the calculation.

Alpha, beta, gamma: diversity across samples

Pool all samples and you have the landscape's diversity, γ. The average within a sample is α. Their ratio, β, says how many distinct communities the landscape effectively holds (Jost 2007):

\[\begin{gathered}\beta_q=\frac{\gamma_q}{\alpha_q}\\[6pt]\alpha_q=\Bigl(\frac1n\sum_{i}\sum_j p_{ij}^{\,q}\Bigr)^{1/(1-q)}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#d0583c}{\beta_q}=\frac{\color{#3d7bd9}{\gamma_q}}{\color{#2f9a55}{\alpha_q}}\]
γ
diversity of everything pooled: the whole landscape or slide set
α
the typical diversity inside one sample
β
how many times bigger the whole is than its typical part

Read it aloud. Total diversity, divided by the average diversity within a sample.

Familiar relative. This is the multiplicative cousin of splitting variance in ANOVA. There, total = among + within (added); here, total = among × within (multiplied). It reads as "the effective number of completely distinct communities".

\[\begin{gathered}SS_{\text{total}}=SS_{\text{among}}+SS_{\text{within}}\\[6pt]\gamma=\beta\times\alpha\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. If each sample holds 10 effective taxa and the pair together holds 15, β = 1.5: one and a half communities' worth of difference.

With two samples of equal weight, β runs from 1 (the samples are identical) to 2 (they share nothing). With n samples it can reach n. Because β is a ratio of Hill numbers it inherits the dial q: β0 counts shared and unshared taxa equally, β2 asks whether the dominants differ.

Try this
  • Shared taxa at 100%: β = 1 at every q.
  • Shared at 0%: β = 2 at every q.
  • Shared at 50% with low evenness: β0 sits halfway, but β2 depends on whether the shared taxa are the dominant ones. Use "Shared taxa are" to flip it.

Sampling effort and rarefaction

Count more grains and you find more taxa, so raw richness confounds diversity with effort. Rarefaction asks how many taxa you would expect in a subsample of n grains drawn from a count of N (Hurlbert 1971):

\[E[S_n]=\sum_{j}\left[1-\frac{\binom{N-N_j}{n}}{\binom{N}{n}}\right]\]
Dissect this equation
\[E[S_n]=\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_j}\Bigl[1-\color{#d0583c}{\frac{\binom{N-N_j}{n}}{\binom{N}{n}}}\Bigr]\]
C(N−Nj, n) / C(N, n)
the chance that n grains drawn without replacement miss taxon j entirely: the ways to pick only from the other grains, over all ways to pick
1 − …
the chance taxon j turns up at least once
Σj
add those chances: the expected number of taxa seen

Read it aloud. For every taxon, take the chance it gets found in n grains, and add those chances up.

Familiar relative. This is the card-dealing (hypergeometric) probability. The chance a 5-card hand has no hearts is C(39, 5)/C(52, 5) = 0.22. Adding probabilities to get an expected count is "linearity of expectation", the same trick that says the expected number of heads in 10 tosses is 10 × 0.5.

\[\begin{gathered}P(\text{no hearts in 5 cards})=\frac{\binom{39}{5}}{\binom{52}{5}}\\[6pt]=\frac{575\,757}{2\,598\,960}=0.22\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. A taxon with 3 grains out of N = 1,000, counting n = 150: the miss chance is about 0.85³ ≈ 0.61, so it is found 39% of the time and contributes 0.39 of a taxon to E[S₁₅₀].

Nj is the count of taxon j. Each term is the chance that taxon j turns up at least once in n draws. The jagged line is one real counting order; the smooth curve is the expectation.

Try this
  • Set the counting sum to 150, Ravn's count, then 300. How many taxa are still being added at each?
  • Lower the evenness. The curve rises more slowly, because most draws land on the same few taxa and rare ones need far more grains.
  • Higher-order Hill numbers (q = 2) settle long before richness does. This is why they are steadier between labs that count to different sums.

Sampling design: plots, transects and what they do to the analysis

Every number in this course comes from samples, and the way they were taken is built into every result. Two field crews in the same forest, one using small random plots and one using a transect across the slope, will produce different α, β and γ, different ordinations and different p-values. Neither is wrong; they are answering different questions.

Plot size (grain)

Bigger plots catch more species, so α rises with plot area, roughly as the power law S = cAz from the curves chapter. Because γ for the whole study changes much less, β = γ/α falls as plots grow. Beta diversity is partly a property of your quadrat size, not just of the landscape. Compare studies only at matching grain, or rarefy to a common area.

Plot shape

A long thin plot of the same area crosses more ground, and therefore more patches and more of the gradient, than a square one. It usually records more species, but mixes communities inside one sample. Squares and circles give the most "pure" sample of a patch.

How many plots, and where

  • Simple random placement is unbiased, but can leave rare habitats unsampled by chance.
  • Systematic grids cover space evenly and are easy to relocate. If the landscape has a regular pattern (ridges, furrows), a grid can fall in step with it.
  • Stratified random placement puts a set number of plots in each habitat or band of the gradient, which guarantees coverage. This is the usual choice when groups will be compared.
  • Belt transects are a line of contiguous plots. Run across a gradient, a transect captures it efficiently: the ordination lines the plots up in gradient order and the Mantel r (below) is strong. Run along a contour, the same effort sees no gradient at all and finds fewer species.

The species accumulation curve (a saturating curve) tells you whether you have enough plots: when it flattens, extra plots add few new species.

What you measure in each plot

Counts of stems (density), percent cover, basal area, frequency (the share of subplots where a species occurs) or presence only. Each gives a differently shaped table. Cover and basal area weight big plants; counts weight small, numerous ones; presence weights everything equally. Pick the distance to match: Jaccard for presence, Bray–Curtis or Hellinger for abundance.

Neighbours aren't independent

Plots close together tend to be similar simply because they are close (spatial autocorrelation). The Mantel test measures this. It correlates the matrix of geographic distances with the matrix of community distances:

\[r_M=\operatorname{corr}\left(d_{\text{space}},\;d_{\text{community}}\right)\]

A strong rM in a transect is expected: that is the gradient. But if plots are treated as independent replicates when they are really clustered neighbours, PERMANOVA and ANOSIM p-values come out too small. This is the pseudoreplication problem (Hurlbert 1984). Spread replicates out, or test with restricted permutations.

Nested plots

Whittaker-style nested designs put small subplots inside larger ones (for example 1, 10 and 100 m²). That gives a species–area curve from every site and lets you compare richness at any grain.

Where pollen and coal balls fit

A pollen sample is also a plot, but one whose footprint you didn't choose. Its "relevant source area" depends on the size of the basin and on how far each taxon's pollen travels (Sugita 1994). A small hollow samples nearby vegetation; a large lake samples the region. A coal-ball peel is the opposite extreme: a plot a few centimetres across, sampling one patch of swamp floor. Comparing the two is a question of grain, and every lesson in this chapter applies.

Try this
  • Keep 12 plots, switch from "Random" to "Transect across the gradient", then to "Transect along a contour". Watch the Mantel r, γ and the ordination's colours: across the gradient they run blue to gold; along a contour they are all one shade.
  • Grow the plot size from small to large. Mean α climbs several-fold and β falls, because γ rises more slowly than α.
  • Stretch the plot shape to long and thin at the same area. A few more species per plot, because each plot now crosses more ground.
  • Raise the number of plots and watch the accumulation curve flatten.

Distance between two samples

Ordination never sees samples, only how far apart each pair is. The distance measure is the definition of "different", and each measure has its own idea of what difference means.

Bray–Curtis: unshared abundance

\[\begin{gathered}d_{BC}=\frac{\sum_j\lvert p_{aj}-p_{bj}\rvert}{\sum_j(p_{aj}+p_{bj})}\\[6pt]=1-\sum_j\min(p_{aj},p_{bj})\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#d0583c}{d_{BC}}=\frac{\color{#3d7bd9}{\sum_j\lvert p_{aj}-p_{bj}\rvert}}{\color{#2f9a55}{2}}\\[6pt]=1-\color{#c08f00}{\sum_j\min(p_{aj},p_{bj})}\end{gathered}\]
Σ|pa − pb|
for each taxon, how far apart the two shares are, added up: total disagreement
2
the largest that total can be with proportions; dividing by it keeps the answer between 0 and 1
Σ min
the part of the two compositions that overlaps (shared abundance)
dBC
the share of abundance that is not shared

Read it aloud. Add up the taxon-by-taxon differences and halve them; equivalently, one minus the overlap.

Familiar relative. On proportions this is half the city-block (Manhattan, L1) distance, the distance a taxi drives on a grid rather than as the crow flies. Statisticians call it the total variation distance between two distributions. Σ min is the old "percentage similarity".

\[\begin{gathered}d_{\text{Manhattan}}=\sum_j\lvert a_j-b_j\rvert\\[6pt]d_{BC}=\tfrac12\,d_{\text{Manhattan}}\ \text{(on proportions)}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. Samples A and B in the table: the differences 0.10, 0.05, 0.15, 0.10, 0.06, 0.04 add to 0.50, so d = 0.25. They share 75% of their composition.

On proportions the denominator is 2, so Bray–Curtis is one minus the overlap, the dark part of each bar pair. It ranges from 0 to 1 and hits 1 as soon as two samples share nothing.

Jaccard: unshared taxa

\[d_J=1-\frac{c}{a+b-c}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#d0583c}{d_J}=1-\frac{\color{#2f9a55}{c}}{\color{#3d7bd9}{a}+\color{#c08f00}{b}-\color{#2f9a55}{c}}\]
a
taxa present in sample a
b
taxa present in sample b
c
taxa present in both; subtracting it once stops them being counted twice
dJ
the share of all taxa present that are not shared

Read it aloud. One minus (shared taxa ÷ all taxa found in either sample).

Familiar relative. A Venn diagram: the overlap divided by the whole area of both circles. Image analysts call it "intersection over union". Its sibling Sørensen, 2c/(a + b), is exactly Bray–Curtis applied to presence–absence data.

\[\begin{gathered}J=\frac{\lvert A\cap B\rvert}{\lvert A\cup B\rvert}\\[6pt]\text{Sørensen}=\frac{2J}{1+J}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. A has 6 taxa and B has 5, all 5 shared: J = 5/(6 + 5 − 5) = 5/6, so d = 0.167.

a and b are each sample's richness, c the taxa they share. Abundance is gone: one grain counts as much as two hundred.

Euclidean and Hellinger: straight-line distance

\[\begin{gathered}d_E=\sqrt{\sum_j(p_{aj}-p_{bj})^2}\\[6pt]d_H=\sqrt{\sum_j\bigl(\sqrt{p_{aj}}-\sqrt{p_{bj}}\bigr)^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#d0583c}{d_E}=\sqrt{\color{#2f9a55}{\sum_j}\color{#3d7bd9}{(p_{aj}-p_{bj})^2}}\\[6pt]d_H=\sqrt{\sum_j(\color{#c08f00}{\sqrt{p_{aj}}-\sqrt{p_{bj}}})^2}\end{gathered}\]
(pa − pb)²
each difference squared, so big differences count far more than small ones
Σ
add across all taxa (all dimensions)
√
undo the squaring to get back to distance units
√p first
Hellinger square-roots the shares before differencing, which pulls dominant taxa down

Read it aloud. Pythagoras in many dimensions: square the gaps, add, take the square root.

Familiar relative. Standard deviation is this same recipe applied to one sample and its mean: square the gaps from the mean, average, take the root. The square root in Hellinger is the classic variance-stabilizing transform for counts, used because a Poisson count's variance equals its mean.

\[\begin{gathered}\text{SD}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\\[6pt]d_E=\sqrt{\sum_j(a_j-b_j)^2}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. For samples A and B, the squared gaps add to 0.0502, so dE = 0.224. After square-rooting the shares, they add to 0.1938, so dH = 0.440.

Euclidean squares the differences, so a single dominant taxon swamps the rest. Hellinger takes square roots first, which shrinks the dominants and lifts the rare. Hellinger is what makes PCA usable on community data (Legendre & Gallagher 2001).

The distance matrix, and why distances saturate

Do this for every pair and you get the distance matrix, the only thing an ordination ever looks at. Here the samples come from one environmental gradient, such as wet to dry. Species rise and fall along it in Gaussian curves (the "coenocline"), and the samples are sorted by their true position.

The right-hand plot holds the key to the next three chapters. Across it is how far apart two samples really are on the gradient; up it is their ecological distance. Close samples behave well, but once two samples share no species, Bray–Curtis is stuck at 1 however much further apart they are. This is saturation. Euclidean distance has no fixed ceiling, but it plateaus just the same: once two samples share nothing, its value depends only on how concentrated each sample is, not on how far apart they lie.

  • Widen it. Distances stay roughly proportional to gradient separation, and every method in the next chapters behaves.
  • Switch to Euclidean with narrow niches. No pair touches a ceiling, yet the cloud is just as flat on the right.

PCA: rotating the axes to catch the most variance

Picture each sample as a point whose coordinates are its taxon abundances, one axis per taxon. With two taxa you can draw it: a cloud of points. PCA finds the direction through the cloud along which the points are most spread out, which is PC1. It then finds the most spread-out direction at right angles to that, PC2, and so on. Nothing is distorted; the axes are only rotated.

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{C}=\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}_c^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c\\[6pt]\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{v}_k=\lambda_k\mathbf{v}_k\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\begin{gathered}\color{#8a5cc4}{\mathbf{C}}=\frac{1}{n-1}\color{#3d7bd9}{\mathbf{X}_c^{\top}\mathbf{X}_c}\\[6pt]\color{#8a5cc4}{\mathbf{C}}\color{#2f9a55}{\mathbf{v}_k}=\color{#d0583c}{\lambda_k}\color{#2f9a55}{\mathbf{v}_k}\end{gathered}\]
XcᵀXc
centred data multiplied by itself: sums of squares on the diagonal, sums of cross-products off it
C
the covariance matrix: variances of each taxon down the diagonal, covariances between pairs elsewhere
vk
a direction that C only stretches, never turns: a principal axis
λk
how much it is stretched, which is the variance of the samples along that axis

Read it aloud. Build the table of variances and covariances, then find the directions it stretches without turning; the stretch is the variance along each.

Familiar relative. With one variable, C is just the variance s² (SD squared). Correlation is covariance divided by both SDs. The eigenvalues add up to the total variance, so "PC1 explains 85%" means λ₁ / Σλ = 0.85.

\[\begin{gathered}\text{var}(x)=s^2=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\\[6pt]r=\frac{\text{cov}(x,y)}{s_x\,s_y}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. Two standardized taxa with correlation ρ: the eigenvalues are 1 + ρ and 1 − ρ. With ρ = 0.7, PC1 holds (1 + 0.7)/2 = 85% of the variance. The PCA figure above lands near that; the exact share differs slightly because its sample of 60 points is random.

The eigenvector v1 of the covariance matrix is PC1's direction, and its eigenvalue λ1 is the variance it captures. Turn the dial to sweep a candidate axis around the cloud. The bar shows the variance along it, and its peak is PC1.

Try this
  • Sweep the angle and find where the variance bar peaks, then press "Snap to PC1" to check yourself.
  • Raise the correlation between the two taxa. PC1 takes nearly everything. Lower it and the variance spreads across both axes.

With real communities the cloud has dozens of dimensions, and along a long gradient it isn't a cloud but a curved, saturating path (previous chapter). PCA can only rotate it, so the path's bend shows up in the plot as the horseshoe. See it in "One gradient, four lenses".

PCoA: from a distance matrix to a map

Principal coordinates analysis starts from any distance matrix and finds points whose straight-line distances match it as closely as possible. It takes four steps:

  1. Square every distance and halve it with a minus sign: aab = −½dab2.
  2. Double-centre the matrix: subtract each row's mean and each column's mean, and add back the grand mean.
  3. Find the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the result, B.
  4. Scale each eigenvector by the square root of its eigenvalue; those are the coordinates.
\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{B}=-\tfrac12\,\mathbf{J}\,\mathbf{D}^{(2)}\mathbf{J}\\[6pt]\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{I}-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\\[6pt]\mathbf{z}_k=\mathbf{v}_k\sqrt{\lambda_k}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#8a5cc4}{\mathbf{B}}=-\tfrac12\,\color{#2f9a55}{\mathbf{J}}\color{#3d7bd9}{\mathbf{D}^{(2)}}\color{#2f9a55}{\mathbf{J}}\]
D(2)
every distance squared
J … J
double-centring: subtract row means and column means, add back the grand mean, which moves the origin to the centroid
B
a table of "dot products" between samples; its diagonal is each sample's squared distance from the centroid

Read it aloud. Square the distances, halve them with a minus sign, and centre them both ways. What's left behaves like a covariance table, so you can read coordinates off its eigenvectors.

Familiar relative. The law of cosines from trigonometry: d² = |a|² + |b|² − 2 a·b. Rearranged, a·b = −½(d² − |a|² − |b|²). Double-centring is what removes the |a|² and |b|² terms. That is how distances alone can be turned back into a map, like triangulating positions from known distances.

\[\begin{gathered}c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\theta\\[6pt]\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=-\tfrac12\bigl(d_{ab}^2-\lVert\mathbf a\rVert^2-\lVert\mathbf b\rVert^2\bigr)\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. The sum of B's diagonal (its trace) is the total sum of squared distances from the centroid, the same SST that PERMANOVA splits up. See "How the formulas are related".

With Euclidean distance, PCoA is PCA. With Bray–Curtis some eigenvalues come out negative (red in the scree plot): no flat space can hold those distances exactly. That doesn't break the method, but it is a warning that the first two axes leave something unrepresented.

One gradient, four lenses

Here is the same set of samples, taken along one gradient, through four methods. Colour runs from the wet end (blue) to the dry end (gold), and a thin line joins the samples in their true order. A perfect ordination would lay them out in a straight line along axis 1, in order.

  • PCA folds the line into a horseshoe, and the two ends curl back toward each other because Euclidean distance saturates: past a point, farther samples look no more different.
  • CA (χ² distance, unimodal model) gives the arch: axis 2 is largely a bent copy of axis 1. DCA was invented to cut this out by detrending.
  • PCoA on Bray–Curtis arches less but still bends, because the distances it honours are saturated.
  • NMDS only keeps rank order, so "saturated" just means "furthest", and it holds the order longest. But once most pairs are tied at the maximum, the ties carry no order and even NMDS folds. vegan's stepacross() was written for this case.

Under each plot, ρ is the rank correlation between axis 1 and the true gradient position. Close to 1 means the order was recovered.

NMDS, one step at a time

Non-metric multidimensional scaling doesn't solve an equation; it searches. It starts from some configuration of points, then repeats three moves:

  1. Measure the distance z between every pair on the plot.
  2. Fit the best monotone step line from the original distances to the plot distances. This is isotonic regression, here by the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm. The fitted values ẑ respect only rank order: if pair 1 is more different than pair 2 in the data, ẑ1 ≥ ẑ2.
  3. Move every point to shrink the gap between z and ẑ (here by the SMACOF update).

Familiar relative. This is a residual standard error expressed as a proportion. In regression, 1 − R² = SSresidual / SStotal. Stress² is built the same way, except the "total" isn't centred on a mean.

With numbers. Stress 0.10 means the plot distances miss their ideal positions by about 10% of a typical plot distance. Stress 0.30 means 30%, which is why the picture becomes unreliable.

Stress by iteration

Stress, random starts and noise

Stress measures one thing: how faithfully the picture keeps the rank order of the original distances. Clarke's (1993) rough guide is below 0.05 excellent, below 0.1 good, below 0.2 usable with care, and above 0.3 close to arbitrary.

Three cautions follow from the mechanics:

  • Local minima. Different starts can end at different stresses. The histogram shows the final stress from 30 random starts. Always keep the best of many, and check that the best repeats.
  • Noise and sample size. More noise, or more samples, usually means more stress, so a fixed cut-off penalizes big datasets (Dexter et al. 2018).
  • Low stress is not separation. A plot can map its distances perfectly and still show no structure; "Do the groups differ?" below shows how to check.
Try this
  • Run 30 starts at zero noise, then at high noise. Watch the histogram widen and shift right.
  • Raise the number of sites in the gradient chapter and rerun.

Do the groups differ? ANOSIM, PERMANOVA, silhouette, hit rate

Now the samples come from three communities on two gradients, as on the bench. The question shifts from "is the map faithful?" to "are these groups really different?" Four answers come from four angles, and all of them work on the same distance matrix.

ANOSIM: ranks between versus within

\[\begin{gathered}R=\frac{\bar r_B-\bar r_W}{M/2}\\[6pt]M=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\end{gathered}\]

\[\color{#d0583c}{R}=\frac{\color{#c08f00}{\bar r_B}-\color{#2f9a55}{\bar r_W}}{\color{#3d7bd9}{M/2}}\]

r̄W

Read it aloud. How much higher, on average, the between-group distances rank than the within-group ones, as a fraction of the most it could be.

Familiar relative. This is the Mann–Whitney U test's idea (do one set's ranks run higher than another's?), applied to distances. Using ranks instead of raw values is like using the median instead of the mean: one extreme value can't drag it around.

With numbers. With 435 pairs, ranks run from 1 to 435. If between-group pairs average rank 290 and within-group 90, R = (290 − 90)/(435/2) = 0.92.

Rank every pairwise distance. If groups are distinct, between-group pairs (red) take the high ranks and within-group pairs (dark) the low ones. R near 1 means complete separation and near 0 none.

Read it aloud. How much closer this sample is to its own group than to the next-best group, as a fraction.

Familiar relative. This is a standardized difference, in the same spirit as an effect size (difference ÷ a scale), but done one sample at a time. Averaging all the si gives a single score for how cleanly the groups separate.

Procrustes: comparing two plots

Two ordinations of the same samples, say from plants and from pollen, can't be compared directly, because NMDS axes have no fixed direction or scale. Procrustes analysis removes everything that doesn't matter, then measures what's left:

  1. Centre both configurations on the origin.
  2. Scale each to a total sum of squares of 1.
  3. Rotate, and reflect if that helps, the second onto the first to minimize the squared distances between matching points.
\[\begin{gathered}m^2=1-\Bigl(\max_{\mathbf Q}\operatorname{tr}\,\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf Y\mathbf Q\Bigr)^2\\[6pt]r=\sqrt{1-m^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#d0583c}{m^2}=1-\Bigl(\max_{\color{#c08f00}{\mathbf Q}}\operatorname{tr}\,\color{#3d7bd9}{\mathbf X^{\top}\mathbf Y}\color{#c08f00}{\mathbf Q}\Bigr)^2\]
XᵀY
the sum, over matching samples, of products of their coordinates in the two plots
Q

tr

Read it aloud. Spin and flip one plot until it lines up best with the other; the leftover mismatch is m², and the agreement is r = √(1 − m²).

Familiar relative. This is Pearson's correlation generalized to points in a plane. Pearson's r is the average product of standardized x and y. Procrustes r is the summed products of standardized coordinates of matching points, after the best rotation. m² plays the role of 1 − r².

\[\begin{gathered}r=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i z_{x,i}\,z_{y,i}\\[6pt]r_{\text{Procrustes}}=\sqrt{1-m^2}\end{gathered}\]

With numbers. r = 0.93 means the pollen plot and the plant plot share about 0.93² ≈ 86% of their configuration; the other 14% is mismatch.

The lines join each sample's two positions after fitting; their total squared length is m2. The Procrustes correlation r is 1 for identical shapes. Step through the stages to see each transformation on its own.

Once you have a plot, you can ask which measured variables line up with it, for example water content, salinity or depth. Regress the variable on the two axis scores:

\[\begin{gathered}y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1 z_{i1}+\beta_2 z_{i2}+\varepsilon_i\\[6pt]R^2=1-\frac{\sum(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2}\end{gathered}\]
Dissect this equation
\[\color{#3d7bd9}{y_i}=\color{#c08f00}{\beta_0}+\color{#d0583c}{\beta_1 z_{i1}+\beta_2 z_{i2}}+\varepsilon_i\]
yi

Familiar relative. This is ordinary multiple regression, with the axis scores as predictors. R² is the share of the variable's variance explained, and it equals the squared Pearson correlation between y and its prediction ŷ. The arrow is the direction of steepest climb on the fitted plane.

How the formulas are related

Nearly every index in this course is a familiar statistic in a new setting. The table pairs each with its relative from an introductory statistics course. The dissections under each formula go into more detail.

One identity carries most of the weight, and it's worth seeing work with your own eyes: the spread of a set of numbers can be measured from their pairwise differences alone, without ever computing the mean.
